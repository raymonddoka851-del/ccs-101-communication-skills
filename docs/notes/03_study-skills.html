03 · Study Skills and Information Literacy

Study focus: plan learning, engage actively, retain and retrieve information, and use academic sources responsibly. Based on Topic 5 study-skills notes and the CCS 101 course outline.

1. Study actively

Studying is learning through reading, reflection, and practice so information can be understood, retained, recalled, and applied. Study skills are the methods and habits that support those steps.

  • Passive learning: mainly listen, reread, or memorize without testing understanding.
  • Active learning: ask questions, connect ideas, evaluate information, explain concepts, and practise retrieval.

A repeatable study session

  1. Choose one specific outcome (for example, explain the communication process without notes).
  2. Preview the material and turn headings into questions.
  3. Study a manageable section using focused reading and useful notes.
  4. Close the material; retrieve the answer aloud or in writing.
  5. Check and correct gaps; use a practice question or example.
  6. Schedule a later review and adjust your plan based on what remains difficult.

2. Read to remember: SQ3R and PQ4R

SQ3R turns reading into a question-and-recall process:

  1. Survey: preview titles, headings, diagrams, and summaries.
  2. Question: make questions from the headings.
  3. Read: look for answers and supporting details.
  4. Recite/Recall: explain the main points without looking.
  5. Review: check the source, correct gaps, and revisit later.

PQ4R adds reflection:

  1. Preview the material.
  2. Question what you need to learn.
  3. Read to find answers.
  4. Reflect on meaning and connections.
  5. Recite key ideas from memory.
  6. Review the material and correct omissions.

Example: For a section on listening barriers, preview the headings, ask “What kinds of barriers are there?”, read for categories and examples, then list each category and one response from memory.

3. Plan time and protect concentration

A study schedule is a time-management plan that assigns study sessions to learning goals. Planning is ongoing: track actual use of time, make a daily or weekly schedule, set mini-deadlines, review completed work, and revise the plan when tasks or deadlines change.

Build a workable schedule

  • List deadlines and the tasks needed to meet them.
  • Estimate time; break large work into smaller sessions.
  • Put demanding or priority work into available focused periods.
  • Include breaks and other commitments.
  • At day’s end, compare planned with completed work and reset priorities.

The course notes describe the Pomodoro technique as 25 minutes of focused work followed by a 5-minute break. Treat this as one possible structure; adjust the interval to the task and your needs.

For concentration, identify the actual distraction (noise, phone, unclear goal, or fatigue) and choose a matching response: change the environment, set a specific mini-goal, remove a distraction, or take an appropriate break.

4. Memory: encode, store, retrieve

The study notes describe three stages:

  • Sensory memory briefly registers incoming stimuli; attention helps select information for further processing.
  • Short-term memory (STM) holds a limited amount for a short time.
  • Long-term memory (LTM) stores learned information for later retrieval.

The practical study cycle is encode → store/organize → retrieve. Make meaning and connections, organize information, then practise recalling it rather than only rereading.

Memory aids from the course notes include:

  • Chunking: group related details into meaningful units.
  • Acronyms or mnemonic sentences: use initials as retrieval cues (for example, the familiar “ROYGBIV” for the rainbow colour sequence).
  • Visual association: connect a term or name to a memorable image.
  • Practice retrieval: explain, write, or answer questions from memory, then check accuracy.

5. Information literacy and ethical use

Information literacy means recognizing an information need, finding relevant information, evaluating it, and using it responsibly. It supports course research, assignments, presentations, and lifelong learning.

Research workflow

  1. Define the need: turn the assignment into a focused research question; note its scope and required source types.
  2. Search deliberately: use suitable library catalogues, databases, books, or other course-relevant resources. Try key terms and related terms; record where you searched.
  3. Evaluate before relying on a source: check who created it, its evidence and references, relevance to your question, currency when dates matter, and its purpose or perspective.
  4. Take traceable notes: distinguish your own ideas from source wording; record author, title, date, and page or location while reading.
  5. Use and acknowledge sources: paraphrase genuinely in your own structure and language; quote exact wording only when useful and mark it as a quotation; cite ideas and words that are not your own using the required course style.
  6. Present findings: organize evidence around the research question and explain how it supports your conclusions.

Plagiarism and cheating are unacceptable under the course outline. A citation does not make an inaccurate claim reliable, and changing a few words does not turn copied wording into a genuine paraphrase.

Active practice

Use one course question to make a one-week study plan with three short sessions. In the first session, create SQ3R questions; in the second, answer them from memory; in the third, check sources and explain one remaining weak point. Separately, find one library or academic source and record its author, purpose, evidence, relevance, and citation details.

Common pitfalls

  • Scheduling study time without naming what you will learn or practise.
  • Rereading as the only revision method, with no recall or application.
  • Treating a search result’s ranking as proof of reliability.
  • Copying notes without recording their source, or paraphrasing too closely.
  • Making a plan once and not adjusting it when deadlines or workload change.
