01 · Fundamentals of Communication

Study focus: what communication is, how meaning moves, why context matters, and how to make messages effective. Based on the CCS 101 course outline and Topic 1 lecture notes.

1. Communication is a process

Communication is the purposeful exchange of ideas, information, knowledge, attitudes, or feelings through shared symbols, signs, words, and behaviour. It is more than sending information: participants interpret meaning and respond.

A practical process model

Participant/source → encode → message → channel → participant/receiver → decode → feedback

  • Participants may send, receive, or alternate roles. Their knowledge, interests, and emotional state affect the exchange.
  • Encoding puts an idea into words, signs, images, or behaviour; decoding interprets those signs.
  • The message is the content; the channel is how it travels (for example, speech, writing, or a graphic).
  • Feedback is the receiver’s verbal, written, or non-verbal response. It helps show whether meaning was understood.
  • Context includes the situation, time and place, physical environment, and participants’ backgrounds and relationships.

Example: A student emails a lecturer to ask for a deadline clarification. The student encodes a specific question in writing; the email is the channel; the lecturer decodes it and replies. If the answer is unclear, the student asks a focused follow-up.

2. Communication principles and types

  • Inescapable: communication occurs at intrapersonal and interpersonal levels; even choosing not to speak can convey a response.
  • Irreversible: once a message is received, it cannot be fully taken back.
  • Dynamic: meaning and interaction can change as participants respond.
  • Contextual: the same words or behaviour can be interpreted differently in different situations.
  • Complicated: communicators make choices about purpose, wording, timing, audience, and channel.

Classify communication in two useful ways:

ClassificationTypesExample
By participantsIntrapersonal; interpersonal (dyadic or small group); public; massThinking through a decision; a conversation; a speech; a broadcast
By channel/formOral/verbal; written; graphic/visual; non-verbalDiscussion; email; chart; gesture or facial expression

Forms can work together. A presentation may combine spoken explanation, slides, and gestures.

3. Make messages effective: the 7 Cs

Use the course’s 7 Cs as a message check:

  1. Clarity: make the intended meaning easy to understand.
  2. Completeness: include the information the audience needs to act.
  3. Conciseness: remove repetition and unnecessary wording.
  4. Consistency: keep details and claims aligned.
  5. Correctness: check facts, names, grammar, and tone.
  6. Consideration: adapt to the audience’s needs and perspective.
  7. Courtesy: communicate respectfully.

Revision example: “Send me that thing soon” is vague. “Please email me the draft report by 3 p.m. Thursday so I can review it” is clearer and more complete.

4. Barriers and practical responses

BarrierHow it interferesUseful response
Noise or interruptionsImportant parts are missedChoose a quieter place; pause and restate key information
Lack of knowledge or unclear wordingTerms are misunderstoodDefine terms; ask a clarifying question
Strong emotion or low interestAttention and interpretation are affectedPause, listen without jumping to conclusions, and return to the purpose
Stereotyping or assumed agreementThe message is filtered through an unfair assumptionCheck the evidence and ask the person what they mean
Difficulty expressing an ideaThe receiver cannot follow the pointPlan the main point and use a suitable example or channel

5. Put it into practice

Before sending or speaking

Active practice

Choose a real campus announcement. Rewrite it for two audiences (for example, first-year students and a course instructor). Name the purpose, context, channel, and likely feedback for each version. Explain which of the 7 Cs you changed and why.

Common pitfalls

  • Treating communication as one-way delivery instead of exchange and feedback.
  • Assuming a message has the same meaning for every audience.
  • Confusing the channel (email) with the message (the request in the email).
  • Using vague wording, stereotypes, or an unsuitable channel without checking the result.
